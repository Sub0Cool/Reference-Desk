# Moral Turpitude Lookup — Phase 2 Batch 2 research (2026-10-12)

**Scope:** California criminal witness impeachment, not immigration CIMT or professional-license discipline.

## Critical legal rule
*People v. Castro* (1985) 38 Cal.3d 301 analyzes felony-conviction impeachment using the least adjudicated elements. *People v. Wheeler* (1992) 4 Cal.4th 284, 295–300 allows independently proven misconduct involving moral turpitude to be considered for impeachment under Evidence Code §352, but a misdemeanor conviction record offered to prove the conduct faces a hearsay objection. Do **not** treat a 'moral turpitude' characterization as automatic admissibility of the conviction document.

## October 12 follow-up: prosecutor review and classification decisions

User's requested policy positions: PC 243(e)(1), PC 273.5 and PC 136.1 should be treated as morally turpitudinous (including misdemeanors); ordinary misdemeanor DUI should be No; VC 20002 should be flagged Yes to err on the side of review. These preferences are **not all equally established by precedential law**, so the lookup explicitly preserves distinctions.

- **PC 273.5:** Already in the database as Yes. *People v. Rodriguez* (1992) 5 Cal.App.4th 1398, 1401–1402, and *People v. Burton* (2015) 243 Cal.App.4th 129 directly support moral turpitude. A misdemeanor disposition changes how impeachment is proved; it doesn't automatically negate morally turpitudinous underlying conduct.
- **PC 243(e)(1):** Retained Conditional, with an explicit office-review presumption that intimate-partner battery may qualify. Unlike 273.5, the statute may be committed by slight offensive touching without injury; no binding subdivision-specific California impeachment decision was verified declaring all 243(e)(1) convictions necessarily turpitudinous. A real estate administrative decision classified this intimate-partner battery as moral turpitude, while a gaming-agency administrative decision distinguished 243(e) from 273.5. Neither constitutes binding California witness-impeachment precedent.
- **PC 136.1(a)(1):** Marked Yes under witness-intimidation reasoning of *People v. Mickle* (1991) 54 Cal.3d 140, 168. *Mickle* addressed threatening witness misconduct, not a conviction under this precise modern subsection; caveat is mandatory.
- **PC 136.1(b)(1):** Kept Conditional. The statute's elements differ from threatening a witness; a license-adjudication determination found subdivision (b)(1) not necessarily morally turpitudinous. A blanket categorical Yes lacks verified controlling support.
- **VC 23152(a)/(b):** Marked No for ordinary misdemeanor offenses. The 2022 uploaded list places misdemeanor DUI under No and distinguishes *People v. Forster* (1994) 29 Cal.App.4th 1746, 1756–1757, which held the **former recidivist felony DUI** (former VC 23175) involves moral turpitude.
- **VC 20002(a):** Marked Yes as a **conservative office review designation**, not a categorical binding appellate holding. *People v. Bautista* (1990) 217 Cal.App.3d 1 concerns **injury** hit-and-run under VC 20001. In an apparently unpublished *People v. Rodriguez* (2016) G052112, the trial court found 20002 morally turpitudinous and the appellate court described the concealment rationale; however the defendant conceded that issue on appeal and the decision instead addressed Evidence Code §352. A State Bar Court decision also reasoned §20002 inherently involves concealment, but that is a different legal context. The user's 2022 PDF names VC 20001 but not 20002.

Uploaded source: *2022 CRIMES OF MORAL TURPITUDE LIST.pdf*, current as March 4, 2022. That document itself cautions that attorney discipline and immigration moral-turpitude standards may differ from witness impeachment. Review the actual authority and any citation history before courtroom use.

Do not describe misdemeanor convictions as automatically admissible impeachment evidence. *People v. Wheeler* (1992) 4 Cal.4th 284 distinguishes proof of underlying conduct, which may be admissible subject to evidentiary safeguards.
