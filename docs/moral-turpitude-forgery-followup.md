# Follow-up findings: forgery and aggravated assault (October 9, 2026)

## Forgery — PC 470(a)
Recommendation: **Yes** for moral turpitude under the felony-conviction impeachment analysis. People v. Flanagan (1986) 185 Cal.App.3d 764, 771 expressly recognizes forgery as an offense involving moral turpitude. Current section 470(a) requires unauthorized signature, knowledge of lack of authority, and intent to defraud (see CALCRIM 1900). People v. Cadogan (2009) 173 Cal.App.4th 1502, 1514–1515 discusses the distinct treatment of misdemeanor forgery misconduct under Wheeler. The database classification has been changed from Conditional to Yes on the audit branch; main remains untouched. Flanagan did not interpret the modern subdivided wording of 470(a), so this is a reasoned statutory-elements application, not a falsely claimed direct modern-subdivision holding.

## Section 245 alternatives
People v. Thomas (1988) 206 Cal.App.3d 689, 695–701 addresses former section 245(a)(1) deadly-weapon assault and treats it as involving moral turpitude. People v. Elwell (1988) 206 Cal.App.3d 171 separately addresses former section 245(a)(1) force likely to produce GBI. People v. Cota (2020), discussing AB 1026 (2011), confirms the later separation into 245(a)(1) for deadly-weapon assault and 245(a)(4) for force-likely-GBI assault. This supports the existing mapping in principle, but historical conviction records must still be checked for the particular theory. The 245(a)(2) firearm form is consistent with the deadly-weapon rationale, but the present database should avoid overstating Thomas as a directly modern subdivision-specific holding.

## Officer battery — PC 243(c)(2)
People v. Lindsay (1989) 209 Cal.App.3d 849, 855–859 considers a former section 243(c) felony involving injury to a peace officer. The existing Conditional warning should remain until historical and modern statutory text are reconciled.
